Marma · the vital points

A hundred and
seven points, mapped
by surgeons.

Marma are the places where, as Suśruta describes them, flesh, vessel, ligament, bone and joint come together. They were charted with great care more than fifteen centuries ago, and the map has been in continuous use ever since.

What follows is that map, the classical procedure that works at these sites, and the modern practice that grew out of both.

The mapCharted with real precision

There are 107, and the arrangement is orderly: forty-four in the limbs, eleven to each; twenty-six in the trunk; thirty-seven above the collarbone. The limbs repeat the same eleven-point series, from the web of the fingers or toes up to the groin or armpit.

Each is graded by significance, and each has a size as well as a place — measured in aṅgula, finger-breadths of the person being treated, so the map scales to whoever is in front of you. On this figure one aṅgula is about 2.2 cm.

What stands out is how well it holds up. The points gather where a vessel or nerve runs close to the surface, where a tendon crosses a joint, where bone is thinly covered. Mapped without dissection as we know it, they still line up with where the large vessels and nerves actually run.

Every point can be inspected — move over one, or tap it, and it names itself and says where it lies. The second row of controls narrows the view to the head, the middle of the body, or the lower body, and the camera follows.

Shown are the 72 that fall on a front view. The remainder are on the back and the back of the head.

SirāvyadhaThe classical procedure

Suśruta gives venesection extraordinary standing — he calls it half of all surgical treatment, the way basti is called half of medicine. Of the seven hundred sirā the texts count in the body, ninety-eight may be opened.

Switch the control to venesection for the sites that fall on a front view. Each is matched to particular complaints: the temple for one-sided headache, four aṅgula above or below the knee for sciatica, the elbow for disorders of the arm.

The reasoning is its own: vitiated blood is being drawn off, and the site is chosen for where that blood has settled.

Knowing when to stopThe clinical eye

The venesection chapters are careful about limits, and the signs they give are ones a practitioner could act on at the bedside:

  • Blood that runs dark and thick at first, then turns bright red — that is the signal to stop.
  • A maximum quantity is set, and it is modest.
  • Suited to the strong and well-prepared; not for the very young or very old, the exhausted, or anyone already depleted.
  • Timing matters — season, weather, and not immediately after food.
  • Faintness or chill means stopping at once and attending to that.

That is someone describing a procedure they had done many times, and had watched closely.

Working the pointsPressure, and the classical means

Marma work in the classical literature is manual: sustained or rhythmic pressure with the thumb or fingers, often with warm oil, sometimes with gentle circular movement. It belongs with abhyanga and the oil therapies rather than apart from them.

Certain sites are also approached by other means the texts set out — agnikarma, the heated probe, at particular points; sirāvyadha where a vessel marma is involved.

MarmapunctureA modern development

Needling at marma points is a twentieth-century development, shaped by practitioners who knew both traditions and brought a fine-needle technique to the Indian point map. It is practised today under several names, including sūchī veda.

Switch to needled today for the points it commonly works on — a practitioner selection drawn from the classical map.

It is worth knowing which parts are which. The map is classical; venesection is classical; needling at these points is recent, and stands on its own practice rather than on textual authority. That is a useful thing to be able to say clearly to a patient who asks.

In practiceWhat to expect

Marma therapy by pressure is gentle, is given in a course rather than a single session, and sits comfortably alongside massage and the oil treatments. Venesection and needling are clinical procedures, given by trained practitioners after assessment.

As with any procedure that breaks the skin, the practical points matter: sterile single-use equipment, a practitioner who has assessed you first, and the standard exclusions — bleeding disorders, anticoagulants, pregnancy, diabetes, or being unwell at the time.

The most durable part of all of this is the anatomy. A map of the body drawn in the first millennium, good enough that it still guides where a careful hand goes today.

Ayurveda is a traditional system of medicine and the claims here are its own. This is descriptive rather than instruction. For a headache that is new, severe, or changing in pattern, have it assessed — and for anything concerning your own health, speak to a qualified professional.
