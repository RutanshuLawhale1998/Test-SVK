The name is the whole description: shiras, head, and dhārā, a continuous flow. Warm liquid is poured onto the forehead from a fixed height, moved slowly back and forth across it, and kept unbroken for the length of the session.

Not much happens, and that is the point. The interesting thing is the continuity — the same stimulus, the same warmth, the same sound, for thirty to forty-five minutes without a break.

Nothing is drawn inside the figure. The liquid is the only thing to follow: down from the vessel, onto the forehead, back over the scalp, through the hole in the table, and into the pot beneath it. Scroll and the view closes in on the forehead, then draws back to take in the whole circuit.

A description of a traditional procedure. Not instructions. Carried out by trained practitioners, and unsuitable for a number of conditions.

The set-upEvery part of it is specified

  • The vessel — dhārā pātra — is a wide shallow brass bowl hung on chains above the forehead, tapering to a small spout. The classical height is four aṅgula, four finger-breadths, which is a personal measure rather than a fixed one.
  • What comes out is a thread, not a pour — fine enough that the vessel lasts the session. It falls between the brows and is moved side to side, never held on one spot.
  • The liquid is warmed and recirculated, so its temperature and the flow stay constant throughout.
  • The body lies flat on the table, face up. A hole is cut through the table at the head end: the run-off falls through it into a vessel below, to be strained, rewarmed and returned to the pot above. That recirculation is why one vessel lasts a whole session.

Shirodhāra is one of four classical ways of applying oil to the head, the group called mūrdha taila. The others differ in how the oil is held there: massaged in, kept on a pad, or retained in a leather collar around the scalp.

Why the foreheadThe claim, and what is known

Ayurveda places the site at sthapani marma, between the brows, and treats the head as the seat of the senses and of prāṇa. Pouring there is held to settle the mind and to act on vāta in the head.

The modern account is different and more modest. A steady, warm, moving pressure on the forehead is a continuous mild stimulus to the trigeminal nerve and the skin, and continuous predictable stimulus of that kind tends to shift the balance of the autonomic nervous system toward the calming side. Small studies report slower heart rate, lower anxiety scores, and changes in EEG toward alpha activity during and after treatment.

Those studies are mostly small and often unblinded — a hard thing to blind, given that you know whether oil is being poured on your head. The direction of effect is consistent; the size of it is not established.

Who it is not forThe exclusions

  • acute fever, and any state with undigested residue — āma
  • recent head injury, and raised intracranial pressure
  • acute sinus or ear infection, and open scalp lesions
  • immediately after a heavy meal
  • severe debility, and the very frail

The practical cautions matter as much: the oil is warm and prolonged, so temperature has to be checked and held, and the eyes have to be covered and kept covered.

AfterwardsWhat follows the session

The oil is left on for a period, then the head is washed with warm water or a herbal powder. Rest is prescribed after, and cold, wind and exertion are avoided for the remainder of the day. A course runs seven to fourteen days rather than a single session.

Ayurveda is a traditional system of medicine and the claims here are its own. For anything concerning your own health, speak to a qualified professional.
