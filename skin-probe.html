A gold probe comes off the coals red hot, touches the skin, and cools as the heat crosses into the tissue and spreads. Watch it travel down through the layers — and watch the probe fade from red back to plain gold as it loses what it was carrying.

This shows heat moving through tissue. Nothing here is charred. The procedure itself does produce a controlled burn at the surface; what is drawn here is the conduction underneath it.

Five layersWhat the heat has to cross

Each layer carries heat differently, and that is what shapes the whole picture.

  • Epidermis — a tenth of a millimetre. Crossed almost at once.
  • Dermis — about two millimetres, and full of blood. Heat spreads well here, and is also carried away fastest.
  • Fat — the insulator. Watch the warmth slow as it arrives, and arrive below it late and weakened.
  • Muscle — conducts well again, so the warmth moves on more freely.
  • Bone — a dense outer shell over a spongy interior. Slow to take heat, and the last to feel it.

This is a real diffusion calculation, not a painted glow: each cell of the grid takes its conductivity from the layer it sits in.

When the probe meets the skin it also sends a front down through the tissue, which you can see travelling ahead of the heat and reaching the bone in under a second. Nothing chars and nothing smokes — the probe is warm, not incendiary.

The warmth is drawn across a full thermal spectrum rather than in one colour, because a single hue cannot show gradation: at one colour, a fifth of the way up and two thirds of the way up look the same. Here the leading edge is violet and blue, the body of it green and yellow, and only the contact itself goes white.

The pulseWhy it cannot travel far

The tissue is not still. There is a pulse at sixty-four beats a minute — the whole block lifts a little on each beat, and the specks drifting through the dermis and fat move with it.

Blood is what limits all of this. It clears heat faster than tissue conducts it, which is why the depth reached settles where it does rather than creeping ever deeper. That cooling is in the calculation, rising and falling with the pulse, even though the vessels themselves are not drawn.

It is also why the deep layers here warm on a compressed timescale. At life-size rates almost nothing below the dermis would change at all.

PressureHow much heat crosses

Try the four settings and watch the dent, the readout, and how far the warmth gets.

  • Light touch — barely in contact. Little crosses, and the front dies away in the fat. The bone never feels it.
  • Unsteady — a wavering hand, so the dent is lumpy and drifts and the heat goes in unevenly. It gets further than a light touch, and still stops short of bone.
  • Steady — firm and even. Broad fronts carry all the way down and the bone warms.
  • Firm — pressed hard. The widest contact, the most delivered, and the deepest reach.

Pressing spreads the contact, and a wider contact hands over more heat. The dent is real: the soft tissue is redrawn compressed into it, so every layer above the bone bends with the surface — epidermis most, then dermis, then fat, then muscle, each a little less than the one above. The bone does not move at all. It is rigid, so it holds its line and the flesh is squashed against it, which is what pressing on a knee actually does.

Red hot to goldThe probe is a thermometer

Its colour is its temperature. It arrives already hot, straight off the coals, and descends that way. As it gives the heat up it passes down through yellow, orange and red, settling toward plain gold once it has little left. Then it is returned to the coals and comes back hot again.

Gold is the classical choice for a reason that shows here: it takes heat up evenly and lets it go evenly, and it does not corrode, so nothing reactive is left behind on the skin.
