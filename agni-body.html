Ayurveda's main idea about digestion is simple: what matters isn't what you eat, it's how well your body can break it down and use it. The thing that does that work is called agni — your digestive fire. Use the three buttons in the corner to make that fire weak, balanced, or too strong, and watch what changes.

This explains a traditional system in its own words. It's not medical advice and it isn't a diagnosis. The body drawing is simplified, not a real anatomy chart.

The mouthIt starts before you swallow

Ayurveda says digestion begins the moment food hits your tongue — saliva starts the job, and tasting the food is part of it, not a bonus.

That's because taste is treated as a clue. If you know what something tastes like, you know something real about what it will do inside you. There are six tastes, and each one nudges the body in a particular direction:

Each dot travelling down the body below is coloured by the taste it came in as. You can follow those colours for a while before the later stages take over.

The stomachStage one of three

Food is broken down in three stages, and Ayurveda names each one after the taste the mixture takes on. In the stomach the mixture turns sweet.

This is also where things go wrong first if your digestion is weak. Press weak fire and watch: some dots go dark, get bigger, slow down, and start sticking to the walls instead of moving on.

That sticky leftover has a name — ama. It's food your body couldn't finish processing. Ayurveda treats it as the starting point of most illness, because it clogs the passages the body needs to move things around. In everyday terms it's the heavy, coated, sluggish feeling after a meal you couldn't digest.

The small intestineHere the food splits in two

Stage two, where the mixture turns sour. This is the busiest part of the whole journey, and the most important, because it's where one mouthful becomes two different things.

The nourishing part gets absorbed and leaves the tube — you can see it branching off sideways and heading up toward the liver. The rest stays in the tube and keeps going down.

Everything from here on is one or the other: the part that becomes you, or the part that leaves.

The channelsWhere the good part goes

Ayurveda says the nourishing part of your food travels through srotas — channels running everywhere in the body. Watch the colour sweep outward along the lines: that slow movement is the flow.

What's actually drawn here is the nervous system: brain, spinal cord, the cord ending around the top of the lower back with a spray of loose roots below it, all 31 pairs of spinal nerves at their real levels, and the named nerves running down each arm and leg — each one dividing again and again into a fine mesh, the way real nerves do. It's drawn that way because it's the branching map of the body people recognise.

Worth being clear that these are two different things. Nerves carry signals; the channels Ayurveda describes carry nourishment, and the old texts weren't mapping nerves. One nerve here really is part of the digestion story though — the vagus, the long one running down from the base of the brain to the stomach, which is how the gut and brain actually talk to each other.

First stop is the liver, which sorts things out. After that the food gets turned into your actual body, one tissue at a time, in a fixed order: fluid, then blood, then muscle, then fat, then bone, then marrow, then reproductive tissue. Each step feeds the next, so a shortfall early on shows up as a shortfall in everything after it.

At the very end of that chain is ojas — the best way to describe it is your reserve of strength. It's what Ayurveda says gives you resilience, steady energy, and resistance to getting sick. You can't eat ojas or buy it. It's whatever is left over after everything else has been done well, which is why the meter in the corner tracks it.

The large intestineThe last stage

Stage three, where the mixture turns pungent. Water gets drawn back out and what remains firms up.

Follow the loop: up the right side, across, then down the left. The view stops moving here, because the food is going up before it comes back down.

Ayurveda considers this stretch the home base of dryness and movement in the body — which is why so many of its treatments for restlessness, bloating, constipation and dry skin are aimed at the colon rather than wherever the symptom is showing up.

WasteGetting rid of things is a job

Ayurveda counts three wastes — stool, urine and sweat — and treats making them as real work your body does, not as leftovers it happens to have.

That's why a practitioner will ask about all three. Not out of thoroughness, but because in this system how well you clear things out tells you as much as what you take in. Good digestion means food goes in, gets used, and moves through. Blocked at any point and it counts as a problem.

The three settingsWhy the fire has to be just right

Try all three buttons and compare the two meters.

Weak fire. Food doesn't get fully broken down. Sticky leftovers build up, less goodness reaches you, and more of the meal simply passes through. This is the common one — the heavy, bloated, no-appetite pattern.

Too strong. The opposite problem, and less obvious. Digestion burns through the food so fast that it starts consuming what it was supposed to be building with. You feel constantly hungry but don't get much out of eating.

Balanced. Only this one fills both meters the right way — most goodness absorbed, least wasted. Note that being too strong is not better than being balanced, which is the whole reason Ayurveda talks about digestion in terms of balance rather than strength.

It also explains why the tradition separates two kinds of remedy: one for building a weak fire back up, and a different one for clearing out leftovers that have already collected. Different problems, different fixes.

The endOut

The tube ends and what's left leaves. That includes anything that was never usable in the first place, plus whatever your digestion couldn't manage — which is why the same meal can go much further in one person than another.

The view has pulled back so you can see the whole thing at once. The colour still sweeping outward through the branches stands for the part of your food that became you. The dull stream going down and out is the part that didn't.
